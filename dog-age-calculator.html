Dog Age Calculator

Forget "multiply by 7" — it was never based on real science. This calculator uses the logarithmic formula from a 2020 canine epigenetic aging study, adjusted for your dog's adult size, because a Chihuahua and a Great Dane do not age at the same rate.

Why the "multiply by 7" rule is wrong

The x7 rule assumes a constant aging rate across a dog's whole life and ignores size entirely — neither holds up. A one-year-old dog isn't a human toddler; most breeds are already sexually mature and physically closer to a human in their late teens to late twenties, depending on size. And a dog's later years compress far more aging into each calendar year than the first ones did.

The formula this calculator uses

For dogs one year and older, the base calculation comes from a 2020 study published in Cell Systems, which compared DNA methylation changes (a molecular marker of biological aging) in Labrador Retrievers to those in humans: human age = 16 × ln(dog age) + 31. That formula alone tracks a medium-sized dog reasonably well. This calculator then adjusts it for adult size, since size is the single biggest factor in how fast a dog ages after early adulthood — toy and small breeds tend to stay biologically younger for longer, while large and giant breeds enter their senior years noticeably earlier.

For puppies under one year old, a single number would suggest false precision the science doesn't support — so the calculator shows the developmental stage instead (see below).

Puppy stages (under 1 year)

Roughly, regardless of size: 0–2 months is the newborn/infant stage; 2–4 months is toddler-like, with rapid learning and teething; 4–6 months brings the "gawky adolescent" growth spurt and the start of sexual maturity for many breeds; 6–12 months is closer to a human teenager — physically capable but not yet fully mature, especially in large and giant breeds, which can take up to 18–24 months to finish growing.

Why size changes the answer so much

Large and giant breeds grow faster and reach physical size sooner, but that accelerated development trades off against longevity — they tend to show age-related changes earlier, often from around 5–6 years old, and have shorter average lifespans than small breeds. A small or toy breed at age 10 is often still active and closer to late-middle-age; a giant breed at the same calendar age is typically well into its senior years. This is also why "senior dog" guidance (see our complete guide to caring for a senior dog) usually gives a size-adjusted age range rather than one number for all dogs.

Frequently asked questions

Is this exact? No calculator is — individual health, breed, neuter status, and genetics all affect aging rate. Treat the result as a well-informed estimate, not a medical fact, and talk to your vet about what matters for your specific dog.

Does neutering or spaying affect the result? It can slightly affect growth and lifespan in some breeds, but there isn't a reliable formula to adjust for it, so this calculator doesn't attempt to.

My mixed-breed dog's size is in between two categories — which do I pick? Use their actual adult weight rather than guessing by breed name; weight is what drives the aging-rate difference, not the label.
