How to Read Dog Food Labels (What Actually Matters)

By David Moreno · Updated September 2026 · 7 min read

Dog food packaging is designed to sell, not to inform — words like "premium," "natural," and "gourmet" have no regulated meaning and can be printed on a bag of food regardless of actual quality. The information that matters is smaller, less flashy, and usually on the back of the bag. Here's what to actually look at.

Start with the AAFCO statement

Look for a line referencing the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO) — something like "formulated to meet the nutritional levels established by the AAFCO Dog Food Nutrient Profiles for [life stage]." This single sentence tells you more than the rest of the marketing on the bag combined: it confirms the food is nutritionally complete for a specific life stage (growth/puppy, adult maintenance, or all life stages) rather than a treat or supplemental food that isn't meant to be a sole diet.

The ingredient list: order matters, but not the way people think

Ingredients are listed by weight before cooking, which is genuinely useful but frequently misread. A fresh meat listed first sounds great, but meat is roughly 70% water — after processing, that same ingredient contributes far less dry weight than it appears to. This isn't a red flag on its own; it just means the ingredient list alone can't tell you exact proportions, and "first ingredient is chicken" is a weaker signal of quality than marketing implies.

What's more useful: checking for a named, specific protein source ("chicken," "salmon") rather than a vague one ("meat," "animal byproduct" with no species named), and looking at the first five ingredients as a group rather than fixating on just the first one.

"By-products" aren't automatically bad Named by-products (like "chicken by-product meal") are organ meats and other parts humans typically don't eat but that are nutritionally valuable — they're not filler or waste by default. The real distinction to watch for is whether the source animal is named at all, not whether the word "by-product" appears.

The Guaranteed Analysis: read percentages carefully

This panel lists minimum percentages of crude protein and fat, and maximum percentages of fiber and moisture. The catch: these are minimums and maximums, not exact values, and — critically — they're listed on an "as fed" basis that includes water content. Comparing a wet food's 10% protein to a dry food's 25% protein this way is misleading, since dry food has so much less water to begin with. To compare fairly across wet and dry foods, you'd need to convert both to a "dry matter basis," which most brands don't do for you on the label.

Ignore these marketing terms entirely

  • "Premium," "gourmet," "super premium." None of these terms are regulated or verified by any body — a bag can use them regardless of ingredient quality.
  • "Natural." Loosely regulated; it mainly restricts synthetic ingredients, not overall nutritional quality.
  • "Human-grade." This one does have a real (if narrow) regulatory meaning if used correctly, but it's about facility and ingredient handling standards, not automatically superior nutrition for your dog.

When ingredient quality actually matters more: specific conditions

For most healthy dogs, a food that carries a valid AAFCO statement for the right life stage and has been performing well for your individual dog matters more than chasing trendy ingredient lists. Ingredient sourcing becomes more important with a diagnosed food sensitivity or allergy, in which case working with your vet on an elimination approach is far more reliable than guessing from labels — our guide to dog food for sensitive stomachs covers this in more detail.

A practical checklist for your next bag

  1. Does it have an AAFCO statement matching your dog's actual life stage?
  2. Are the first five ingredients naming specific sources, not vague catch-alls?
  3. Is it made by a manufacturer with an established quality-control and recall track record?
  4. Is your dog actually doing well on it — stable weight, good coat, normal stool — regardless of how the bag markets itself?

That last point matters more than any of the others combined. Label-reading narrows your options to reasonable ones; how your individual dog actually does on the food is the real test.
