Caring for a Senior Dog: A Complete Guide

By David Moreno · Updated September 2026 · 8 min read

"Senior" doesn't arrive on the same birthday for every dog — a Great Dane might get there at 6, while your neighbor's terrier is still acting like a puppy at 10. Whenever it hits, it brings real changes, and you're much better off getting ahead of them than reacting after the fact.

Increase veterinary visit frequency

Move from annual to twice-yearly wellness exams if your vet recommends it — age-related conditions (kidney function, thyroid, arthritis, dental disease) often progress meaningfully in six months, and catching changes early usually means more treatment options and better outcomes.

Watch for the early signs of joint pain

Slowing down on stairs, hesitating before jumping onto furniture, stiffness after resting that improves with movement, and reluctance on longer walks are common early signs of arthritis — often mistaken for a dog "just getting old and lazy" rather than something manageable. Orthopedic bedding (see our crate and bed guide) and, when appropriate, a vet-guided joint supplement or medication plan can meaningfully improve comfort.

Adjust exercise, don't eliminate it

Staying moving is genuinely protective for joint health and cognitive function in senior dogs — the goal is adjusting intensity (shorter, more frequent walks instead of one long one) rather than stopping activity altogether, within what's comfortable for your specific dog.

Nutrition shifts

Many senior dogs need fewer calories as activity and metabolism slow, alongside continued adequate protein (contrary to an older, now largely outdated belief that senior dogs need less protein — current guidance generally favors maintaining protein levels to support muscle mass). See our senior nutrition notes for more on formulas that combine digestive and joint support.

Watch the mind, not just the body Confusion, a flipped sleep schedule, accidents from a dog who's been reliable for years, less interest in things they used to love — these can be early signs of canine cognitive dysfunction, something like dementia in dogs. It gets written off as "just old age" far too often. It's worth a real conversation with your vet.

Home modifications that help

  • Non-slip rugs or runners on hardwood or tile, where reduced traction can cause falls or discourage a senior dog from moving around confidently.
  • Ramps or steps for furniture and vehicles, rather than jumping, which becomes harder on aging joints.
  • Raised food and water bowls, which can ease strain for dogs with neck or joint discomfort.
  • Keeping a consistent layout — moving furniture disorients senior dogs with declining vision more than younger dogs.

Emotional wellbeing matters too

Senior dogs can develop increased anxiety around routine changes, and may show more clinginess or, alternately, more withdrawal. Gentle, patient handling of these shifts — without forcing high-energy interaction they may no longer want — supports their emotional wellbeing as much as any physical care does. See our guide to the Five Domains Model for a fuller framework on quality of life across every dimension, not just physical health.

This guide is for general informational purposes. Any noticeable change in a senior dog's mobility, appetite, cognition, or behavior warrants a veterinary evaluation rather than being assumed to be normal aging.
