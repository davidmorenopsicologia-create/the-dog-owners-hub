How to Crate Train a Puppy: A Step-by-Step Guide

By David Moreno · Updated July 2026 · 7 min read

A crate done right isn't a cage — it's a den. A small, cozy spot your puppy actually chooses to retreat to. Dogs are wired to want that kind of space, which is exactly why this works so well when you introduce it the right way. Done badly — used as a timeout spot, or left in for hours on end — it turns into the opposite: somewhere your puppy dreads. The difference comes down almost entirely to how you introduce it, not the crate itself.

Give this one to two weeks. Rushing it is the single most common way it goes wrong.

Pick the right crate, in the right spot

Size trips up more new owners than you'd think. Too big, and your puppy will just use one corner as a bathroom and sleep in the other — which works against everything you're trying to teach. You want just enough room to stand, turn around, and lie down. If you bought a crate sized for their adult self, a divider panel shrinks the usable space while they're small.

And put it somewhere they're not alone — a corner of the living room, not a garage or basement. Puppies are social animals. A crate in an isolated room fights against the whole point of making it feel safe.

Week 1: Build a positive association

  • Leave the door open at first. Toss treats inside and let your puppy wander in and out freely. Never close the door during this phase.
  • Feed meals in the crate, door open, so the crate becomes associated with something your puppy already loves.
  • Add a worn t-shirt of yours or a familiar blanket — scent is a powerful comfort cue for puppies.

Week 2: close the door, just for a bit

Once your puppy walks in on their own without hesitating, start closing the door — 30 to 60 seconds at first, and stay in the room. From there, stretch it out slowly, and start stepping out of sight for a few seconds before coming back.

The one rule that actually matters Don't let your puppy out while they're mid-whine or mid-bark. That teaches them protesting works, and once they learn that, it's hard to unlearn. Wait for a beat of quiet, then let them out calmly. This one habit is the difference between crate training that sticks and crate training that quietly falls apart.

Working up to real hours

Stretch the time gradually over the following days until you reach what you'll actually need — a workday, say. Remember the bladder math: roughly one hour per month of age, plus one. A three-month-old needs a break within about four hours, crate or no crate. And the crate should never stand in for exercise, food, or bathroom breaks — it's a place to rest, not a place to be parked.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Rushing the timeline — pushing to a full night before your puppy is ready almost always causes a setback.
  • Using the crate as punishment, which undoes the positive association you've built.
  • Leaving a puppy in the crate for hours with no water access during waking hours.
  • Ignoring genuine distress. Whining tied to a real need (a full bladder, real fear) is different from a puppy testing whether protesting gets them out — read your puppy's specific signals rather than applying a blanket rule.

How long until it clicks

Most puppies settle in within one to three weeks. If yours is still in full panic mode — nonstop barking, scratching at the door, real distress — after several weeks of you doing everything gradually and right, that's worth a conversation with a vet or certified trainer. Sometimes it's not about the crate at all; it's an early sign of separation anxiety that needs its own plan.

This guide is for general informational purposes. If your puppy shows signs of significant distress, self-injury, or if you're unsure how to proceed, consult a licensed veterinarian or a certified professional dog trainer.
