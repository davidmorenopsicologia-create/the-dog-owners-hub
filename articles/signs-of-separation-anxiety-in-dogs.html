Signs of Separation Anxiety in Dogs

By David Moreno · Updated September 2026 · 7 min read

Coming home to a chewed-up shoe doesn't automatically mean your dog has separation anxiety. Real separation-related distress is closer to a panic response, and mixing it up with ordinary boredom means you end up fixing the wrong thing — more toys won't touch genuine anxiety, and treating everyday boredom like a clinical disorder just adds stress you don't need.

The signs that point to real separation anxiety

  • It starts almost immediately — often within the first 5-15 minutes of being left, not gradually building over hours.
  • Destruction concentrated at exit points — doors, windows, and the areas around them, rather than general household destruction throughout the day.
  • Vocalizing that continues the entire time you're gone, not just briefly at the start.
  • Pacing, drooling, or panting that isn't explained by heat or exercise.
  • Attempts to escape — scratching at doors or windows severely enough to cause self-injury (broken nails, bleeding paws, damaged teeth from chewing at crate bars).
  • No appetite for food or treats left behind, even ones the dog normally loves — a strong sign of genuine distress rather than boredom.

What it usually isn't

Chewing a shoe here and there, occasional barking that fades after a few minutes, or general restlessness in an under-exercised dog is much more likely to be boredom or excess energy — see our guide to excessive barking for that distinction in more detail.

How to confirm it

Set up a phone or camera pointed at your dog and leave for a short trip (even just stepping outside for 15-20 minutes). Reviewing the footage — specifically how quickly distress starts and how it changes over time — gives you far more reliable information than guessing based on what you find when you get home.

Getting angry about the mess makes it worse, not better It's the most natural reaction in the world — you walk in, see the damage, and you're upset. But a dog with real separation anxiety wasn't misbehaving. They were panicking. Getting angry after the fact doesn't connect to anything in their mind — it happened while you were gone — and it can teach them to dread your return on top of already dreading your absence.

A structured approach that helps

The core method trainers use is gradual desensitization to departures: practicing very short absences (sometimes literally seconds at first) that stay under your dog's anxiety threshold, and slowly extending the time as your dog stays calm. This is a slow process — often weeks — and rushing it by jumping to a full workday too soon typically causes a setback rather than progress.

In the meantime, departure cues (grabbing keys, putting on shoes) can be practiced separately from actually leaving, so they stop predicting distress on their own. A consistent pre-departure routine that ends in something calm, rather than an emotional goodbye, also tends to help.

When to get professional help

True separation anxiety, especially with self-injury or severe destruction, typically benefits from working with a certified professional dog trainer or veterinary behaviorist — in more severe cases, a veterinarian may discuss whether short-term medication support alongside behavior modification is appropriate. This isn't a sign that training "failed" — it's a legitimate, well-documented approach for more intense cases.

This guide is for general informational purposes and is not a substitute for a professional assessment. If your dog shows signs of self-injury or severe distress, consult a veterinarian or certified professional dog trainer.
