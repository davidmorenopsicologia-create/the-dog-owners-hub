Do Dogs Feel Guilt? What the Research Actually Shows

By David Moreno · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Almost every dog owner has seen "the guilty look" — ears back, avoiding eye contact, slinking away — and assumed their dog knows they did something wrong. The actual research on this is genuinely interesting, and the honest answer is more complicated, and more useful, than a simple yes or no.

The study that changed how researchers think about this

A frequently cited study by researcher Alexandra Horowitz set up a scenario where owners were told their dog had eaten a forbidden treat while they were out of the room — sometimes true, sometimes not, regardless of what the dog had actually done. The finding: dogs showed the classic "guilty look" behaviors more in response to being scolded by their owner than to whether they'd actually broken the rule. Dogs who hadn't touched the treat but were scolded anyway showed just as much "guilty" body language as dogs who genuinely had.

What this suggests is actually happening

The "guilty look" appears to be primarily a response to the owner's tone, body language, and history of scolding in that context — an appeasement display responding to a person's displeasure — rather than an expression of the dog understanding they broke a specific rule and feeling remorse about it in the way a person would. This doesn't necessarily mean dogs have zero capacity for anything resembling guilt, but the specific expression owners point to as evidence isn't reliable proof of it.

This connects to a broader question about complex emotions in dogs

Guilt, in the way humans experience it, requires a fairly sophisticated cognitive ability: understanding a rule existed, recognizing you violated it, and connecting that violation to a sense of having done wrong — a chain of reasoning that current research hasn't clearly demonstrated in dogs. What's much better supported is that dogs experience primary emotions clearly — fear, joy, anxiety, affection — and are, as covered in our guide to how dogs read human body language, extremely skilled at picking up on and responding to a person's emotional state.

Why this matters practically, not just academically

If a "guilty look" is actually a response to your reaction rather than evidence of the dog understanding what they did wrong, then scolding a dog for something discovered after the fact — a chewed shoe found an hour later, for instance — teaches nothing useful about the original behavior. The dog can't connect a delayed consequence to an earlier action; all the scolding accomplishes is triggering appeasement behavior in response to your anger in that moment, which looks like understanding but isn't.

What actually works instead

Addressing unwanted behavior requires catching it in the moment (or, more effectively, preventing it through management) and reinforcing the desired behavior — not a delayed reaction to evidence found later. If you come home to a chewed item, the useful response is preventing access to that item going forward, not a conversation the dog can't meaningfully connect to what happened.

A more accurate way to think about the hangdog look

Rather than "my dog knows they did something wrong," a more accurate read is "my dog has noticed I'm upset and is trying to de-escalate the situation" — which is still a genuinely sophisticated social skill, just a different one than guilt. Recognizing that distinction leads to more effective, and arguably fairer, training decisions.
