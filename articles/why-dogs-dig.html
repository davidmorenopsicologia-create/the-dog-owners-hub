Why Dogs Dig, and How to Redirect It

By David Moreno · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

A freshly dug-up flower bed is frustrating, but digging is rarely about spite or defiance — it's almost always one of a handful of specific, identifiable motivations, and each one has a different fix. Figuring out which one applies to your dog matters more than any generic "stop digging" advice.

Temperature regulation

Dug soil is cooler than surface ground in summer and can trap warmth in winter — some dogs dig specifically to create a spot to lie in that's more comfortable than the ambient temperature. This is especially common in double-coated or heavy-coated breeds during hot weather. If digging happens mostly in shaded or cool corners of the yard, temperature is a likely driver — providing shade, a raised cot bed, or supervised time indoors during peak heat often reduces it directly.

Boredom and excess energy

A dog left alone in a yard with nothing else to do will often invent their own activity, and digging is a satisfying, self-rewarding one — it engages the nose, uses energy, and often turns something up (roots, bugs, interesting smells). More structured exercise and mental enrichment before unsupervised yard time, rather than after digging has already happened, tends to reduce this type.

Instinctive, breed-linked digging

Terriers were bred over generations specifically to dig and pursue prey underground, and many still carry a strong instinctive pull toward it regardless of how they're raised. For these dogs, digging isn't a behavior problem to eliminate so much as a need to redirect — trying to fully suppress it in a heavily instinct-driven breed is often an uphill, frustrating battle for both dog and owner.

Prey and pest pursuit

Digging concentrated in one specific, recurring spot — rather than scattered randomly — often means the dog is following a scent to something underground: moles, voles, or other burrowing animals. Addressing the actual pest (humanely, and safely for the dog) usually resolves this type faster than any training approach aimed at the digging itself.

Escape-motivated digging

Digging along a fence line is a different category worth taking seriously — it often signals a dog trying to get out, whether from anxiety, an interesting smell or sound outside the yard, or simple boredom. This type needs a safety response (burying L-footers or pavers along the fence base, and looking honestly at what's motivating the escape attempt) rather than a redirection strategy alone.

Give the behavior somewhere to go

For dogs with a real digging drive, a designated digging pit — a sandbox-style area with loose soil or sand, where treats or toys are buried to make it rewarding — channels the instinct instead of fighting it. Praise and reward digging there specifically, and calmly redirect (not punish) any digging that starts elsewhere by interrupting and walking the dog to the approved spot.

What doesn't work well

Punishing a dog after finding a hole — long after the digging happened — teaches nothing useful, since dogs don't connect a delayed consequence to the earlier action; it mostly just adds anxiety. Filling holes with the dog's own feces, a folk remedy some owners try, isn't reliably effective and creates its own hygiene problems.
