Teaching a Dog to Settle on a Mat or Bed

By David Moreno · Updated October 2026 · 6 min read

A reliable "settle" or "place" command — a dog going to a specific spot and genuinely relaxing there, not just technically staying — is one of the most underrated training investments an owner can make. It's the skill behind a calm dinner guest, a peaceful work-from-home day, and a dog who isn't underfoot every time something interesting happens in the house.

Settle is different from a basic stay

A stay is about a dog holding a position; a settle is about a dog actually relaxing — often lying down fully, sometimes on their side, in a state closer to rest than alert waiting. Training for genuine relaxation, not just physical stillness, is what makes this command useful for long durations, like an entire dinner or a work call, rather than the few minutes a basic stay typically holds up for.

Choose a specific mat or bed as the target

Using a specific, portable mat rather than just "the floor" gives the behavior a clear, consistent target the dog can learn to recognize anywhere — including eventually at a café table or a friend's house, since the mat itself becomes the cue rather than a particular room.

Step 1: build a positive association with the mat

Place the mat down and reward any interaction with it — sniffing, a paw on it, eventually all four feet. No command yet, just building value for being on the mat through repeated, generous rewarding of any voluntary engagement with it.

Step 2: reward lying down on the mat

Once the dog reliably goes to the mat, start rewarding specifically for lying down there, waiting them out if needed rather than luring every time (a dog that offers the behavior rather than being lured learns it more durably). Add the verbal cue ("settle," "place," or whatever word you choose) once the behavior is happening reliably, not before — labeling a behavior that isn't consistent yet weakens the cue's meaning.

Step 3: build duration gradually

Start rewarding for just a couple of seconds of lying calmly, then gradually stretch the time between rewards — a few seconds, then ten, then thirty, working up slowly rather than jumping straight to five minutes. If the dog gets up early, that's useful information that duration increased too fast, not a reason for correction — just reset and shorten the next rep.

Step 4: add real-world distractions

Once duration is solid in a quiet setting, practice during actual household activity — cooking, a video call, family movement around the room — starting with milder distractions and working up, exactly like the distraction-dial approach used in recall training. This is the stage that makes the command genuinely useful, since a settle that only works in silence isn't much help during dinner or when guests arrive.

Using it in daily life

Bring the mat to situations where calm behavior matters — mealtimes, guests arriving, a work call — and send the dog to it as a specific, well-rehearsed request rather than a vague hope they'll calm down on their own. Over time, many dogs start going to their settle spot proactively in these situations, once it's become a strongly reinforced habit rather than just a command.
