New Puppy Checklist: Everything You Need Before Bringing Them Home

By David Moreno · Updated August 2026 · 7 min read

It's easy to overspend on a long list of "must-haves" before a puppy comes home, and just as easy to forget something you'll actually need on day one. This checklist is organized by what genuinely matters in the first 48 hours versus what can wait a week or two.

Before they arrive: the non-negotiables

  • Food — ideally the same brand and formula the breeder or shelter was already feeding, to avoid stomach upset during an already stressful transition. Switch gradually later if you want to change food.
  • A properly sized crate — see our crate training guide for sizing details.
  • A well-fitted collar or harness, and a 6-foot leash — avoid retractable leashes for a new puppy; they make it much harder to control sudden movements near hazards.
  • Food and water bowls — stainless steel or ceramic hold up better than plastic and are easier to keep clean.
  • Puppy pads or a designated potty spot already scoped out — see our potty training schedule guide.
  • ID tag with a current phone number, ready before the first walk.

Puppy-proofing your home

Get down to the puppy's eye level in each room before they arrive — it reveals hazards you'd otherwise miss. Priorities:

  • Secure or remove electrical cords within reach; puppies chew almost everything during teething.
  • Move houseplants that are toxic to dogs (a quick search for "ASPCA toxic plant list" gives a reliable reference) out of reach.
  • Store cleaning products, medications, and anything small enough to swallow (coins, rubber bands, socks) well out of reach.
  • Use baby gates to block off rooms with more hazards or nicer furniture until house training is more reliable.
  • Check your yard for gaps in fencing — puppies are more determined and more flexible than most owners expect.

Worth buying vs. worth skipping

Worth it: a few durable chew toys appropriate for teething (rather than one expensive toy that gets destroyed in a day), an enzymatic cleaner specifically made for pet accidents (regular cleaners don't fully remove the scent, which can encourage repeat accidents in the same spot), and a baby gate if you don't already puppy-proof the whole house.

Skip for now: an elaborate wardrobe of outfits, a large bed sized for their adult size (a smaller one is easier for them to feel secure in, and cheaper to replace once they outgrow it), and most "starter kits" that bundle a dozen items — you'll usually find you only use two or three of them.

The first vet visit

Schedule this within the first few days, even if the puppy seems perfectly healthy. It establishes a baseline, confirms the vaccination schedule from the breeder or shelter is on track, and gives you a chance to ask questions specific to your puppy's breed and history.

The one thing most new owners underestimate Time. Not money — time. The first two weeks involve far more supervision than people expect: near-constant bathroom monitoring, managing a new sleep schedule, and a puppy that genuinely cannot be left unsupervised in an unproofed room for more than a few minutes. Clearing your calendar for the first week, if you can, makes a real difference in how smoothly the transition goes.

Settling in: the first 48 hours

Keep the first two days low-key. Limit visitors, avoid overwhelming outings, and let your puppy explore one room at a time rather than the whole house at once. A quiet, predictable start helps a puppy settle faster than an exciting welcome party would.

This guide is for general informational purposes and is not a substitute for guidance from your veterinarian, who can advise on your specific puppy's needs.
