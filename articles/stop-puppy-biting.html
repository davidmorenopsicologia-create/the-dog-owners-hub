How to Stop a Puppy From Biting

By David Moreno · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

Almost every puppy bites — technically, "mouths" — during their first several months. It isn't aggression. Puppies explore the world with their mouths the way toddlers explore with their hands, and biting is also how they learned to play with littermates. The goal isn't to eliminate the instinct overnight; it's to teach your puppy what an acceptable outlet looks like, and how hard is too hard.

Why puppies bite: three overlapping reasons

  • Teething. Between roughly 3 and 6 months, sore gums make chewing genuinely relieving.
  • Play. Mouthing was how they wrestled and communicated with littermates — it's a default play behavior, not misbehavior.
  • Overstimulation or overtiredness. A lot of "out of nowhere" biting spikes happen when a puppy is overtired, in what many owners call the "witching hour."

The core technique: bite inhibition through feedback

Puppies learn how hard is too hard the same way they learned it from littermates: through feedback. When a puppy bites too hard during play with another puppy, the bitten puppy yelps and stops playing — an immediate, clear consequence.

You can replicate this:

  1. The moment teeth touch skin too hard, give a short, sharp "ow!" — not a scream, just enough to interrupt.
  2. Immediately stop all interaction for 10–15 seconds. Turn away, remove your hand, disengage.
  3. Resume play calmly. If the biting happens again, repeat — consistency matters more than intensity.
What not to do Avoid yelling, tapping the nose, "alpha rolling," or holding the muzzle shut. These methods don't teach bite inhibition — they teach a puppy that hands are unpredictable or scary, which can create fear-based reactivity later, including defensive biting. Research on dog training consistently shows reward-based methods outperform punishment-based ones for both effectiveness and the dog's welfare.

Redirect, don't just correct

Interrupting the bite is only half the plan — you also need to give your puppy something appropriate to bite instead. Keep a soft toy or rope within reach during play sessions, and the moment you interrupt a bite on skin, offer the toy. Puppies that bite from teething discomfort often respond well to a chilled (not frozen) rubber chew toy.

Manage the environment while the habit forms

Bite inhibition takes weeks to fully develop, so managing the situation matters as much as correcting it in the moment:

  • Watch for early overtiredness signs and start a calm wind-down routine before the "witching hour" hits.
  • Keep short, structured play sessions rather than long unstructured free-for-alls, which tend to escalate mouthing.
  • Make sure your puppy is getting enough physical and mental exercise for their age — an under-stimulated puppy bites more, not less.

When it's more than typical puppy mouthing

Most puppies noticeably improve within 4 to 8 weeks of consistent redirection. If biting is accompanied by growling, stiff body language, or seems driven by fear rather than play — or if it hasn't improved with consistent practice by around 6 months — it's worth consulting a certified professional dog trainer or a veterinary behaviorist rather than continuing to manage it alone.

This guide is for general informational purposes and describes typical puppy mouthing behavior. If you're concerned about aggression rather than play biting, consult a licensed veterinarian or certified veterinary behaviorist.
