How to Stop a Puppy From Biting

By David Moreno · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

Your puppy is not aggressive. That needle-sharp mouth attached to your hand every five minutes? That's just how puppies are — technically it's "mouthing," not biting, and almost every single one does it. They explore the world mouth-first the way a toddler explores with their hands, and it's also just how they used to play with their littermates. You're not trying to erase the instinct — you're teaching them what's okay to bite, and how gently.

Three reasons it's happening

  • Teething. Between roughly 3 and 6 months, sore gums make chewing genuinely relieving.
  • Play. Mouthing was how they wrestled and communicated with littermates — it's a default play behavior, not misbehavior.
  • Overstimulation or overtiredness. A lot of "out of nowhere" biting spikes happen when a puppy is overtired, in what many owners call the "witching hour."

Teach it the way littermates did

Here's how puppies actually learn "too hard": another puppy yelps and stops playing. Simple, immediate, unmistakable. You can be that feedback loop:

  1. The moment teeth touch skin too hard, give a short, sharp "ow!" — not a scream, just enough to interrupt.
  2. Immediately stop all interaction for 10–15 seconds. Turn away, remove your hand, disengage.
  3. Resume play calmly. If the biting happens again, repeat — consistency matters more than intensity.
Skip these, even though they're tempting Yelling, tapping the nose, pinning them down, holding the muzzle shut — none of it teaches bite inhibition. What it teaches is that hands are scary and unpredictable, and that can turn into real fear-based reactivity down the line, including defensive biting. The research on this is pretty consistent: reward-based methods beat punishment, both for results and for your dog's wellbeing.

Give them something better to bite

Stopping the bite is only half the job — you need a "yes" to go with every "no." Keep a soft toy or rope nearby during play, and the second you interrupt a bite on skin, swap in the toy. If teething is the culprit, a chilled (not frozen) rubber chew often does the trick.

Set the stage so it happens less

Bite inhibition doesn't click overnight — it takes weeks. In the meantime, a few things help a lot:

  • Watch for early signs of overtiredness and start winding things down before the "witching hour" hits.
  • Keep play sessions short and structured. Long free-for-alls tend to spiral into more biting, not less.
  • Make sure they're getting enough exercise, physical and mental. An under-stimulated puppy bites more, not less — it's often an energy problem wearing a training-problem costume.

When it's not just puppy stuff anymore

Most puppies get noticeably better within 4 to 8 weeks of consistent redirection. If you're seeing growling, stiff body language, or it feels more like fear than play — or nothing's improved by around 6 months — that's the point to bring in a certified trainer or veterinary behaviorist instead of continuing to handle it solo.

This guide is for general informational purposes and describes typical puppy mouthing behavior. If you're concerned about aggression rather than play biting, consult a licensed veterinarian or certified veterinary behaviorist.
