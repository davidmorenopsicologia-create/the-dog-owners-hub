Best Training Treats for Dogs: What to Look For

By David Moreno · Updated September 2026 · 7 min read

Grab whatever bag is closest on the shelf and you're probably setting your training session up to fail before it starts. Not every treat pulls its weight — here's what actually matters, and which kind earns its place for which situation.

What makes a treat "high value" for training

  • Small size. Pea-sized or smaller, so your dog can eat it in one bite without pausing training to chew.
  • Strong smell. Soft, aromatic treats hold attention better than dry, low-odor biscuits — especially in distracting environments.
  • Soft texture. Crumbly or hard treats slow down rep speed and can be messier to carry and dispense quickly.
  • Distinct from everyday food. Save the most exciting treats for training specifically, so they retain their "special" status.

Match the treat to the difficulty of the task

Trainers commonly work with a simple two-tier system:

  • Everyday treats (kibble-based training treats, plain biscuits) — fine for practicing well-known behaviors in a low-distraction environment, like a quiet living room.
  • High-value treats (freeze-dried liver or meat, small pieces of cooked chicken or hot dog, string cheese) — reserved for harder scenarios: a new skill, a distracting environment, or recall training around other dogs.

Here's the mistake almost everyone makes: using an everyday treat for a hard task, then deciding your dog "just isn't food motivated." Nine times out of ten, the dog's fine — the reward just wasn't good enough to beat whatever else was competing for their attention.

Treat categories worth knowing

  • Freeze-dried single-ingredient treats (liver, meat): very high value, low mess, long shelf life. Often the best choice for recall and other high-distraction training.
  • Soft commercial training treats: convenient and mess-free for everyday practice, but check the ingredient list — some are mostly fillers with low actual meat content.
  • Human-food options (small pieces of plain cooked chicken, small cubes of cheese): often the highest value of all, and useful in moderation, but should stay a minority of daily calories and avoid anything seasoned, fatty, or on the list of foods unsafe for dogs (onion, garlic, grapes, and chocolate, among others).
  • Long-lasting chews (not bite-sized): useful for independent settling and crate association, but too slow for active training reps.

Calorie management for treat-heavy training

A rule of thumb worth following Treats — including training treats — should make up no more than about 10% of a dog's daily calories. During an intensive training phase (like a new puppy learning many commands), reduce your dog's regular meal portions slightly to compensate, or switch to using a portion of their actual kibble as the training reward for lower-stakes reps.

What to check on the label before buying

  • A named protein source high on the ingredient list (e.g., "chicken," not vague "meat by-products").
  • Minimal artificial colors or preservatives — these add nothing to training value.
  • An appropriate size for your dog — many bags include a large "biscuit" size that isn't ideal for rapid-fire training reps and needs to be broken up.
