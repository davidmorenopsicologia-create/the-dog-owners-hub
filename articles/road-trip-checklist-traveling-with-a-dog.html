Road Trip Checklist for Traveling With a Dog

By David Moreno · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

A road trip with a dog can go smoothly with a bit of preparation, or become a stressful scramble without it. This checklist covers the practical essentials, including a few things first-time dog travelers commonly forget until they're already on the road.

Restraint and vehicle safety come first

An unrestrained dog in a moving vehicle is a genuine safety risk — both as a projectile in a sudden stop and as a potential driving distraction. A crash-tested dog seatbelt harness, a secured travel crate, or a properly installed vehicle barrier for the cargo area are the main options; a dog loose in the front seat or hanging out an open window, however common it looks, isn't a safe way to travel.

Before you leave: the essentials list

  • Food (enough for the full trip, plus a buffer, since travel food can be hard to find away from home) and any medications, with dosing schedule written down
  • A collapsible water bowl and enough water, especially if traveling somewhere the tap water will be unfamiliar to your dog's stomach
  • Waste bags — more than you think you'll need
  • Current ID tag and confirmation that microchip information is up to date, in case of separation somewhere unfamiliar
  • A recent photo of your dog on your phone, useful if you ever need to describe or search for them
  • Leash, harness, and a backup leash in case one is lost or damaged
  • Familiar bedding or a favorite toy, which helps with unfamiliar sleeping environments
  • Copies of vaccination records, especially for any state or international border crossings, boarding stops, or unexpected vet visits

Plan rest stops like you would for a small child

Roughly every 2-3 hours for bathroom breaks, water, and a short walk to stretch — longer stretches without a break increase both physical discomfort and the odds of an accident or restlessness that becomes a distraction while driving.

Never leave a dog alone in a parked car

This bears repeating specifically for road trips, where a quick stop for food or gas can tempt owners to leave a dog "just for a minute" — vehicle interior temperature can become dangerous far faster than expected in both hot and, to a lesser extent, cold weather (see our guide to hot weather safety). Plan stops around drive-throughs, outdoor seating, or a second person staying with the dog instead.

Managing car anxiety or motion sickness

For dogs prone to car anxiety or nausea, short practice trips in the weeks before a long one — building up gradually from a five-minute drive to longer ones — help build tolerance far more effectively than a first long trip being the actual trip. Feeding a light meal a few hours before departure, rather than right before, can reduce motion sickness; ask your vet about anti-nausea medication if it's a persistent issue.

Finding pet-friendly stops in advance

Confirming pet policies for hotels, rest areas, and any planned stops before departure avoids finding out mid-trip that a booked accommodation doesn't actually allow dogs, or discovering unexpected size or breed restrictions.

A first-aid basics kit

A small kit with gauze, self-adhesive bandage wrap, tweezers, a digital thermometer, and the phone number for a 24-hour emergency vet along your route (looked up in advance, not searched for in a stressful moment) rounds out a genuinely prepared trip.
