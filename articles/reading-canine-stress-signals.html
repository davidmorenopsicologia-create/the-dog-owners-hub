Reading Canine Stress Signals: A Behavior Science Guide

By David Moreno · Updated August 2026 · 8 min read

A growl or a bite rarely comes out of nowhere. Dogs typically communicate discomfort through a whole sequence of subtler signals first — what behaviorists sometimes call the "ladder of aggression" — and a growl is usually one of the last, loudest rungs, not the first. Learning to read the quieter signals earlier in that sequence lets you intervene before a dog feels the need to escalate.

Early, subtle signals

These are easy to miss because they look almost neutral out of context:

  • Lip licking when no food is present — a common low-level stress signal, especially when it happens quickly and repeatedly.
  • Yawning outside of tiredness — like lip licking, often a self-soothing signal rather than sleepiness.
  • "Whale eye" — the whites of the eyes visible in a crescent shape, usually when a dog is looking sideways at something without turning their head, signaling discomfort with a person, animal, or object nearby.
  • A closed mouth after previously panting — a sudden shift from relaxed, open-mouthed panting to a tightly closed mouth often signals rising tension.
  • Turning the head or body away from a person or dog — a polite, low-intensity request for space.

Mid-level signals

  • Freezing — a completely still body, often held for a second or two, is a serious signal that's easy to underestimate because it looks calm.
  • A tucked tail or lowered body posture — general fear or submission, distinct from a relaxed, loosely wagging tail.
  • Excessive shaking off (like shaking off water) when not wet — often a release of tension after a stressful interaction.
  • Pinned-back ears — context matters here, but combined with other signals on this list, usually indicates discomfort.

Late-stage signals

By the time a dog is growling, showing teeth, snapping, or lunging, they've often already tried several of the signals above — sometimes for the exact same reason, but they went unnoticed or, worse, were scolded (see the callout below on why that specifically backfires). A growl is information, not defiance: it's telling you exactly where the dog's threshold is.

Why punishing a growl is dangerous, not just unkind Growling is one of a dog's clearest, most honest warning signals. Punishing it (yelling, a physical correction) doesn't remove the underlying discomfort — it just teaches the dog that warning you isn't safe. Dogs punished for growling don't necessarily become less stressed; they often skip the warning next time and go straight to a bite, because the growl itself got them in trouble. Address the cause of the stress, not the signal telling you about it.

What to do when you spot early signals

Increase distance from whatever is causing the stress, if possible. Avoid forcing continued interaction ("he needs to get used to it") in the moment — that tends to increase stress rather than resolve it. Note the specific trigger (a particular type of person, a loud noise, another dog's body language) so you can work on gradual, positive exposure later, ideally with guidance from a certified trainer if the trigger is frequent or intense.

Context always matters

No single signal in isolation is a reliable diagnosis — a single yawn might just be a yawn. It's the combination of signals, the situation, and what's happening around the dog that gives you the real picture. Getting to know your own dog's individual baseline (how they look and act when genuinely relaxed) is what makes these signals meaningful rather than a checklist applied blindly.

This guide is for general informational purposes. If your dog shows frequent stress signals, growling, or any bite history, consult a certified professional dog trainer or veterinary behaviorist for an individualized assessment.
