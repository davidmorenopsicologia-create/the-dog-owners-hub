Best Small Dog Breeds for Any Household

By David Moreno · Updated September 2026 · 8 min read

"Small" tells you almost nothing about who you're bringing home. A Cavalier and a Miniature Schnauzer can both fit in a tote bag and have almost nothing else in common — energy, patience with kids, how independent they are. Picking by size alone is one of the most common reasons small-dog adoptions don't work out. Here's what each of these popular breeds is actually like to live with.

Cavalier King Charles Spaniel

Consistently gentle and eager to please, Cavaliers are among the most reliably good-natured small breeds with children and other pets. They crave companionship and don't do well left alone for long stretches — a better fit for a household with someone home most of the day than for a demanding work schedule.

French Bulldog

Low-energy and famously well-suited to apartment living, "Frenchies" need minimal exercise compared to most breeds. Their flat-faced (brachycephalic) structure means they're prone to breathing difficulty in heat and shouldn't be over-exercised — a genuine health consideration to research before choosing the breed, not just a personality trait.

Cardigan and Pembroke Welsh Corgi

Don't let the short legs fool you — Corgis are working dogs with real herding instincts and genuine exercise needs. They're intelligent, trainable, and good with children, but tend to be more independent and vocal than some other small breeds, and their herding instinct can show up as nipping at heels if not redirected early.

Boston Terrier

Often called "the American Gentleman," Bostons are friendly, adaptable, and generally good with kids and other dogs. They're moderately energetic — enough to need daily walks and play, but not so much that apartment life is a problem.

Miniature Schnauzer

Alert, intelligent, and low-shedding (a real plus for allergy-sensitive households), Schnauzers are also more vocal and terrier-like in temperament than some owners expect from a "small dog" — they were bred as ratters, and that instinct still shows up as a strong prey drive and a tendency to bark at anything unfamiliar.

Choosing by household, not just by breed name

Questions worth answering before you choose How many hours a day will the dog realistically be alone? Are there young children, and if so, how much active supervision can you guarantee during early interactions? Do you have other pets, and how does this breed typically respond to them? Individual temperament varies within any breed — these guides describe tendencies, not guarantees, and meeting an individual dog (or asking a reputable breeder or shelter about their specific personality) matters more than the breed label alone.

A note on where you get your dog

Whichever breed you're drawn to, reputable breeders who health-test their dogs, and rescue organizations or shelters that assess temperament before adoption, are both far better paths than pet stores or unverified online sellers — which are more often linked to poor breeding practices and undisclosed health or behavior issues.
