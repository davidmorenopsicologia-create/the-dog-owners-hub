Should You Get a Second Dog? What to Consider First

By David Moreno · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

"Dogs do better in pairs" is repeated often enough that it's treated as settled fact, but it's really a case-by-case question — a second dog can be a wonderful addition or a genuinely difficult one, and the difference usually comes down to a handful of predictable factors worth thinking through honestly before deciding.

Your current dog's temperament matters more than the idea of companionship

A dog who's generally social, plays well with others at the park or in classes, and shows relaxed body language around new dogs is a much safer bet for a smooth introduction than a dog who's territorial, has shown resource guarding, or is generally more reserved around other dogs. Adding a second dog doesn't reliably "fix" an anxious or reactive dog's issues — it more often adds a new variable on top of an existing one.

Age and energy level pairing

A young, high-energy puppy paired with an older, lower-energy dog can go two ways: either the puppy provides welcome stimulation, or the puppy's constant pestering becomes a genuine source of stress for a senior dog who no longer wants that level of interaction. Watching how your current dog actually responds to energetic young dogs elsewhere — not assuming they'll love it — is a better predictor than instinct alone.

The real time and financial cost, honestly assessed

A second dog roughly doubles most recurring costs — food, vet care, boarding, grooming — see our cost-of-ownership breakdown to estimate what a realistic second dog budget looks like on top of your current one. Training time also doesn't simply split in half; each dog still needs individual training attention, and managing two dogs simultaneously during walks, guests, or vet visits takes real additional skill and time, especially early on.

Introducing dogs matters as much as deciding to get one

How the first meeting and first weeks go has an outsized effect on the long-term relationship between the two dogs — a rushed, poorly managed introduction can create lasting tension that's much harder to undo than it would have been to prevent. See our guide to introducing a new dog to a resident dog for the specific steps that make this go well.

Consider matching, not just "another dog"

Two dogs with wildly mismatched play styles (a rough, physical player and a dog who prefers gentle interaction) or wildly mismatched exercise needs can end up coexisting rather than genuinely bonding. When possible, meeting a potential second dog with your current dog before committing — many shelters and rescues actively encourage this — gives real information a breed description or age alone can't.

Signs it might not be the right time

  • Your current dog is still actively working through a behavior issue (reactivity, anxiety, resource guarding) that hasn't stabilized yet.
  • Your household is going through a major transition (move, new baby, job change) that already limits the time and consistency a second dog's adjustment period needs.
  • You're considering it primarily to solve a problem with the current dog (loneliness, boredom, anxiety) rather than as an addition both dogs are likely to benefit from.
