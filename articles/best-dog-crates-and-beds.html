Best Dog Crates and Beds, By Size and Age

By David Moreno · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

Forget brand and material for a second — size is what actually makes or breaks a crate or a bed. Get it wrong and even the fanciest option on the market works against you, whether that's crate training that stalls or sleep that isn't actually restful.

Crate sizing

A crate should be just large enough for your dog to stand up, turn around, and lie down flat — not larger. An oversized crate lets a puppy use one end as a bathroom and the other to sleep, working directly against house training (see our crate training guide for the full method). For a growing puppy, a crate with a divider panel lets you buy one adult-sized crate and shrink the usable space as needed, rather than buying multiple crates as they grow.

Crate types

  • Wire crates — good airflow and visibility, foldable for travel, generally the most versatile choice for most homes.
  • Plastic (airline-style) crates — more den-like and enclosed, often preferred by anxious dogs who want less visual stimulation, and required for most airline travel.
  • Soft-sided crates — lightweight and portable, best for calm, already crate-trained dogs rather than puppies or dogs prone to chewing or scratching at barriers.

Bed sizing and type by life stage

Puppies

An inexpensive, washable bed is the right call — accidents and chewing are common at this stage, and an expensive bed is likely to get destroyed or soiled before it's earned its keep.

Adults

Bed size should allow a dog to fully stretch out in their preferred sleep position, with a few extra inches of margin — measure your dog lying down at full stretch rather than guessing from their standing height.

Senior dogs

Orthopedic memory foam beds provide meaningfully more joint support than standard fill, which matters more as arthritis and joint stiffness become more common — this is one of the clearer cases where a specific product feature (supportive, pressure-relieving foam, not just "senior" marketing) has a real functional benefit. Lower-profile beds with easy step-in access also help dogs with reduced mobility or vision.

One crate mistake that undoes months of training Moving to a larger crate "because they've grown" without re-establishing the same training principles (door open first, positive associations rebuilt) can cause setbacks — a new, unfamiliar space isn't automatically the safe den the old crate became through weeks of positive experience. Reintroduce a new crate the same gradual way you introduced the first one.

Placement matters as much as the product

Both crate and bed should go somewhere central to household activity rather than an isolated room — dogs are highly social, and a bed or crate tucked away from where the family spends time gets used far less, regardless of how comfortable it is.

This guide is for general informational purposes. Dogs with existing joint conditions may benefit from a veterinarian's specific recommendation on bed support and firmness.
