Best Dog Breeds for Apartment Living

By David Moreno · Updated August 2026 · 7 min read

Size is the first thing everyone checks when picking an apartment dog — and it's almost the least useful one. A 70-pound Greyhound will happily nap on your couch all afternoon. A scrappy 15-pound terrier bred to hunt vermin can turn a one-bedroom into a nonstop circus. What actually predicts a good fit for small spaces is energy level, how much a breed likes to sound off, and whether it was bred to work alone or stay glued to you.

What to actually screen for

  • Exercise needs that match what you can realistically provide — not what you aspire to provide on your best week.
  • Barking tendency — some breeds were developed specifically to alert-bark (many terriers, some herding breeds); others were bred to be quiet working or companion dogs.
  • Tolerance for alone time — apartment living often means more hours alone during the workday than a house with a yard might.
  • Size relative to your specific space — still matters, just less than most people assume, and mostly for logistics (getting a large dog up and down stairs multiple times a day, for example).

10 breeds that tend to do well in apartments

  • Greyhound — despite their size, often called "45 mph couch potatoes"; low daily exercise needs once past puppyhood, and generally quiet indoors.
  • French Bulldog — low exercise needs and generally calm indoors, though brachycephalic breathing means care with heat and exertion (see a vet's guidance on this before assuming any low-energy small breed is automatically "easy").
  • Cavalier King Charles Spaniel — affectionate, adaptable, moderate exercise needs, generally good with apartment noise levels.
  • Basenji — famously "barkless" (they make other vocalizations instead), independent, moderate energy.
  • Bichon Frise — small, adaptable, generally good-natured with neighbors and other pets.
  • Shih Tzu — bred as a companion breed, low exercise needs, generally calm indoors.
  • Whippet — similar profile to Greyhounds: capable of short bursts of speed, but content to lounge the rest of the day.
  • Bulldog — low energy and generally quiet, with the same heat-and-exertion caveats as other brachycephalic breeds.
  • Havanese — small, adaptable, bred as a companion breed with generally moderate barking.
  • Poodle (Toy or Miniature) — highly trainable, which makes managing apartment-specific behaviors (barking, greeting manners) considerably easier regardless of size.

The breeds that usually struggle here, and why

Border Collies, Australian Shepherds, most sled breeds — these dogs were built for hours of hard physical and mental work every day. Take that outlet away and the energy has to go somewhere: destruction, barking, anxiety. Not because the dog is difficult, but because the apartment simply can't meet a need that's wired into them. It's not impossible with a genuinely committed owner and serious daily exercise — but be honest with yourself about that commitment before you bring one home.

Mixed breeds and shelter dogs Breed lists are useful for setting expectations, but a huge number of great apartment dogs are mixed breed or breed-unknown shelter dogs. Ask shelter staff about the individual dog's actual energy level and behavior in their care — real observed behavior is a better predictor than a guess at breed mix from appearance alone.

What matters more than breed

Regardless of breed, a consistent daily routine (walks, mental stimulation, a predictable schedule) does more to prevent apartment-specific problems like barking and destructive behavior than breed selection alone. See our guide to excessive barking if noise is already a concern with a dog you have.

This guide is for general informational purposes. Individual temperament varies within any breed — meet a specific dog, and ideally its parents if available, before assuming breed traits will hold true.
