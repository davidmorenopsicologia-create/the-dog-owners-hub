Best Dog Training Collars, By Size and Temperament

By David Moreno · Updated August 2026 · 7 min read

There's no single "best" collar — despite what every listicle titled "Best Dog Collars 2026" wants you to believe. The right one depends on your dog's size, how hard they pull today, and their temperament. Here's what actually fits which situation.

Flat collars

Best for: dogs that already walk reasonably well on leash, and for holding ID tags day-to-day.

A standard buckle or quick-release collar is fine for everyday wear and light leash use, but it isn't designed to manage a dog that pulls hard — sustained pressure on the neck from pulling can strain the trachea over time, especially in small breeds prone to tracheal sensitivity.

Martingale collars

Best for: dogs with narrow heads relative to their neck (Greyhounds, Whippets, many sighthounds) that can back out of a flat collar.

A martingale tightens slightly when the dog pulls back, without the choking action of a true choke chain, making it a safer no-slip option specifically for breeds prone to slipping their collar. It's not primarily a pulling-correction tool — it's an escape-prevention one.

Front-clip harnesses

Best for: dogs that pull moderately to strongly, and puppies still learning leash manners.

Clipping the leash at the chest rather than the back redirects a pulling dog's momentum sideways instead of letting them drive forward with full body weight, which makes pulling far less rewarding without any pain-based correction. This is generally the best starting point for most dogs learning not to pull, including puppies.

Back-clip harnesses

Best for: dogs that already walk well, or breeds prone to neck sensitivity (small brachycephalic breeds, dogs with a history of tracheal issues) regardless of pulling habits.

Comfortable for daily wear, but a back-clip harness gives a strong-pulling dog effectively free rein to lean into it — for a dog that pulls hard, this often makes the problem worse rather than better, since it removes friction rather than adding it.

Head halters (Gentle Leader, Halti-style)

Best for: larger, strong dogs with an established, hard-pulling habit that a front-clip harness alone hasn't resolved.

These give the most control with the least physical effort by guiding the head rather than the body — where the head goes, the body tends to follow. They require a proper introduction period, since many dogs paw at their face the first few times wearing one; rushing this step is the most common reason owners give up on head halters.

What to skip entirely Choke chains and prong collars work by making pulling uncomfortable, and yes, that can look like fast results. But there's a real, documented risk of physical injury behind that speed, and for some dogs it makes anxiety or reactivity worse instead of fixing the pulling. A front-clip harness or head halter, paired with real leash training (our training treats guide covers what to reward with), gets you the same outcome without the risk.

Sizing tips that actually matter

Whatever you choose, you should be able to fit two fingers flat between the collar or harness strap and your dog's body — snug enough that it can't slip off, loose enough that it isn't restrictive. Re-check the fit every few weeks on a growing puppy; outgrowing gear happens faster than most new owners expect.

This guide is for general informational purposes. If your dog has a history of neck, back, or tracheal issues, ask your veterinarian which type of collar or harness is appropriate before making a change.
